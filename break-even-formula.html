How to work out your break-even cost per hour

The whole formula, every input, and a worked example. Copy it into your own sheet if you would rather build it than buy it — that is a completely reasonable choice.

Break-even cost per hour is the amount an hour of your working time costs you before you have made a single cent. Quote below it and the job costs you money to do. Quote at it and you have worked for nothing. Everything above it is your actual profit.

It is the number that should sit under every quote you send, and most operators have never calculated it.

The formula

Break-even / hour =
(annual fixed + annual variable + your wage)
÷ annual billable hours
The trap is in the denominator. It is billable hours, not worked hours.

Step 1 — Annual fixed costs

Everything you pay whether or not you work this month.

Everything that scales with the work you do.

Step 3 — Your own wage

This is the step people skip, and skipping it is how a business quietly becomes a job that pays less than a job.

Put in what you would have to pay somebody competent to do your work. Not what is left over — what the role is worth. If the business cannot cover that number, you need to know now, not in three years.

Owner wage at $28/hr × 1,800 worked hours$50,400

Step 4 — Billable hours, not worked hours

Here is where most calculations go wrong. You work 1,800 hours a year. You do not invoice 1,800 hours.

Hours worked per year1,800
Billable ratio (six billed in a ten-hour day)60%
Billable hours per year1,080

Step 5 — Divide

Fixed$8,865
Variable$7,600
Owner wage$50,400
Total annual cost$66,865
÷ billable hours1,080
$61.91 / hour
Break-even. Below this the job costs you money. At this you worked for your wage and nothing more.

Step 6 — Your minimum billable rate

Break-even is not a price. It is a floor. Add the margin the business needs to survive a bad month, replace a machine early, or grow.

Break-even$61.91
+ 25% target margin$15.48
Minimum billable rate$77.39

And now the uncomfortable part. If that number comes out above the going rate in your area, the arithmetic is not wrong. It is telling you the problem is upstream: too few billable hours per day, too much driving, an owner wage the current volume cannot support, or a mix of work that is too cheap.

That is a genuinely useful thing to learn in August. It is a terrible thing to learn in January.
